

\section{IOT System Reliability and Evaluation}

This section evaluates the reliability and performance of the distributed communication architecture described in Chapters~\ref{chap:implementation} and earlier. Because the two computational nodes are bridged across the public internet rather than a shared LAN, the central design claims made for the architecture --- low latency and full ROS2 QoS fidelity --- are treated here as hypotheses to be verified experimentally. The evaluation mirrors the layers of the architecture (the ZeroTier overlay and the Fast~DDS real-time channel), followed by an end-to-end assessment and a qualitative discussion of failure modes and resilience.

In this context, \textit{reliability} is defined as the system's ability to deliver data correctly and on time over an inherently unreliable public-internet path.

\subsection{Evaluation Methodology}

The communication stack was instrumented and measured under nominal operating conditions, with the two laptops connected over the live ZeroTier overlay running ROS2~Jazzy and the \texttt{rmw\_fastrtps\_cpp} middleware. The following tools were used:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Network-layer measurement:} \texttt{ping} for round-trip latency, jitter, and loss over the ZeroTier virtual interface, and \texttt{iperf3} for available throughput.
    \item \textbf{Middleware-layer measurement:} a pair of timestamped ROS2 ping--pong nodes that measure round-trip --- and hence one-way --- message latency on a single clock, so that any clock offset between the two machines cancels out. The same nodes report message-delivery counts, giving the drop rate, and were run under both Reliable and Best-Effort QoS.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Network-Layer Reliability (ZeroTier Overlay)}

The overlay is the foundation on which the communication stack runs, so its characteristics bound the performance of everything above it. The measured latency, jitter, packet loss, and throughput over the ZeroTier virtual interface are reported in Table~\ref{tab:iot_kpi} and illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:zerotier_latency}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/chapter7/zerotier_latency.png}
    \caption{Round-trip latency and jitter measured over the ZeroTier overlay during continuous operation.}
    \label{fig:zerotier_latency}
\end{figure}

Over a continuous 2000-packet probe (one packet every 200\,ms), the overlay sustained an average round-trip time of 46.3\,ms with \textbf{zero packet loss}, confirming that the ZeroTier path between the two laptops is stable enough to carry real-time telemetry. The distribution is, however, distinctly long-tailed: the median RTT is only 14.2\,ms, yet the 95th percentile rises to 184\,ms and the worst-case sample reaches 467\,ms, with the corresponding jitter (\texttt{mdev}) at 47.7\,ms. This skew is characteristic of a public-internet path subject to transient queueing and scheduling delays rather than of any fault in the overlay itself, and it motivates the QoS analysis that follows: a delivery policy must tolerate occasional large latency excursions without compromising correctness. Available throughput over the tunnel measured 4.5\,Mbps, which --- while modest compared with a LAN --- comfortably exceeds the bandwidth required by the brick-detection, \texttt{/joint\_states}, and status streams, so the link is latency-bound rather than bandwidth-bound for this application. These figures substantiate the Chapter~5 claim that end-to-end performance is governed by the underlying internet path and the overlay's tunnelling overhead rather than by any middleware-level inefficiency.

\subsection{Real-Time Channel Reliability (Fast DDS / RTPS)}

This subsection evaluates the DDS channel that carries the live telemetry. The channel was characterized with a controlled \texttt{std\_msgs/Header} probe topic published at 20\,Hz, measuring end-to-end message latency and the message-drop rate under each of the two DDS delivery contracts.

\subsubsection{Quality-of-Service Comparison}

The key experiment contrasts the two delivery contracts available through DDS QoS: \textbf{Reliable} versus \textbf{Best-Effort}. This empirically grounds the QoS discussion of Chapter~5 by quantifying the latency-versus-loss behaviour of the two policies over the live overlay.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Latency and message loss under Reliable vs.\ Best-Effort QoS.}
    \label{tab:qos_comparison}
    \begin{tabular}{lcccc}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Probe Topic} & \textbf{Target Rate} & \textbf{QoS} & \textbf{Mean Latency (ms)} & \textbf{Drop Rate (\%)} \\
        \midrule
        Telemetry probe (\texttt{Header}) & 20\,Hz & Reliable     & 6.14 & 0.00 \\
        Telemetry probe (\texttt{Header}) & 20\,Hz & Best-Effort  & 6.82 & 0.00 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

On the overlay both policies delivered all 1000 messages without loss, so the two contracts are separated here only by latency. Reliable QoS achieved a mean one-way latency of 6.14\,ms with a tight 95th-percentile RTT of 15.3\,ms, whereas Best-Effort was marginally slower in the mean (6.82\,ms one-way) and noticeably more variable at the tail, with the 95th-percentile RTT rising to 22.0\,ms and a worst-case RTT of 156\,ms against Reliable's 50\,ms. This ordering may appear counter-intuitive --- Best-Effort is often assumed to be the lower-latency option --- but on a link with negligible loss the acknowledgement traffic of the Reliable profile imposes no retransmission penalty, while its in-order delivery yields a smoother latency distribution. Reliable QoS is therefore retained as the default for the telemetry streams: at no measurable latency cost over this path, it additionally guarantees delivery, which is the property the architecture depends on whenever the underlying internet path is less forgiving than it was during these measurements.

\subsection{End-to-End System Reliability}

Finally, the architecture was assessed as experienced by the robotic application. The \textbf{digital-twin synchronization lag} --- how far the on-screen twin trails the physical arm's true joint state --- is given directly by the one-way latency of the \texttt{/joint\_states} stream measured above, namely 6.14\,ms under Reliable QoS. A lag of this magnitude is far below the threshold of human perception and well within the tolerance of the supervisory workflow, so the operator's view of the remote cell is effectively synchronous with the physical robot.

\subsection{Failure Modes and Resilience Discussion}

The quantitative results above are complemented by a qualitative analysis of how the architecture behaves when the public-internet path misbehaves. The principal failure modes anticipated for this deployment are: transient internet outages, latency spikes, packet loss, NAT rebinding, fallback of the ZeroTier path from direct to relayed routing, and interruptions caused by a laptop entering and resuming from sleep.

Against these, the architecture provides several layers of built-in resilience, each of which can be cross-referenced to a design decision made in Chapters~5 and~6:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reliable-QoS retransmission} in DDS recovers individual lost packets on the real-time channel without application involvement.
    \item \textbf{ZeroTier's stable virtual IPs and automatic relay fallback} preserve addressing and connectivity across NAT changes and direct-path failures.
    \item \textbf{Static unicast discovery} removes the dependency on multicast, so discovery does not silently fail across the WAN boundary.
\end{itemize}

The measured nominal performance --- zero packet loss, single-digit-millisecond DDS latency, and a stable overlay --- confirms that, in the common case, these mechanisms keep the link transparent to the robotic application. The long latency tail observed at the network layer is precisely the kind of disturbance that the Reliable-QoS retransmission path is designed to absorb without loss, which is why that policy is adopted for the telemetry streams that the supervisory workflow depends on.
